# Build Skip-Bo as a DOM-rendered, synthesized Electron game

Build the game as a pure-TypeScript rules engine wrapped in a DOM/SVG/CSS front end inside Electron 45. All card art is SVG generated in code. All sound, including music, is synthesized with raw Web Audio. The engine is a deterministic reducer that holds the official Mattel rules (162 cards, a 30-card stock for 2-4 players and 20 for 5-6, a hand of 5, four shared building piles and four discard piles per player). It exposes every Mattel-adjacent variation as a configuration field. One AI engine drives three difficulty tiers by changing parameters, not code. The same engine is what makes the testing strategy work: the rules, the AI and the audio event mapping all test in plain Node with seeded randomness, and Playwright covers only the thin Electron shell. The stack is Electron 45, electron-vite 6, Vite 8, Vitest 5, Playwright 1.64 and electron-builder 26. Build the macOS universal DMG locally and build Windows on a `windows-latest` CI runner. The main caveat is that much of the art, sound and AI tuning is design judgment. The researchers found no authoritative sources for those areas, so every number below is a starting value to playtest. The rules themselves are firmly sourced from Mattel.

## The official rules are small, sourced and mostly unambiguous

The deck is **162 cards: twelve each of 1-12 plus 18 Skip-Bo wilds** ([Mattel 42050](https://service.mattel.com/instruction_sheets/42050-0920.pdf), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skip-Bo)). Mattel gives only the totals of 144 numbered cards and 18 wilds. The per-number count of 12 comes from Wikipedia and arithmetic. The dealer deals **30 cards to each player for 2-4 players and 20 for 5 or more**, face down as the personal stock pile with the top card turned up ([Mattel R7311](https://service.mattel.com/instruction_sheets/R7311-0920.pdf)). That rule gives a 102-card draw pile at 2 players and 72 at 3. The 4-player draw pile has 42 cards, and the 5-player one has 62. The 6-player draw pile has 42 cards. All of these comfortably cover the hands. Mattel's quick game deals 10 stock cards each and changes nothing else ([Mattel 42050](https://service.mattel.com/instruction_sheets/42050-0920.pdf)).

A turn runs as follows, per [Mattel R7311](https://service.mattel.com/instruction_sheets/R7311-0920.pdf) and [N7808](https://service.mattel.com/instruction_sheets/N7808.pdf). The player draws up to 5 cards. They may then play any number of cards onto the up-to-four shared building piles from the hand, the top of their stock, or the top of any of their own discard piles. If the hand is emptied, they draw 5 more and keep playing. The turn ends only by discarding one hand card onto one of four personal discard piles. Only the top discard card is playable. Only a 1 or a Skip-Bo can start a building pile. Piles build 1 through 12 and are removed when complete ([Mattel 42050](https://service.mattel.com/instruction_sheets/42050-0920.pdf)). When the draw pile runs out, the completed building piles are shuffled into a new one ([Mattel N7808](https://service.mattel.com/instruction_sheets/N7808.pdf)). **The first player to empty their stock wins immediately**, even holding cards in hand. Optional match scoring awards 25 points plus 5 per card left in each opponent's stock, with the match going to the first player to 500 ([Mattel R7311](https://service.mattel.com/instruction_sheets/R7311-0920.pdf)). The Mattel sheets compared (42050, R7311, N7808) carry identical rules. The only difference is a typo in N7808.

Three digital decisions fall out of the rules. First, a wild's value is fully determined by the target pile (top plus one), so the UI needs no value-picker. The player just chooses the pile. A wild placed on top of an 11 completes the pile. Second, Mattel is silent on an empty draw pile with no completed piles to recycle. The researchers' recommended handling is to draw what is available. If the hand is then empty, the turn ends without a discard. Treat this as a documented design decision and test it explicitly. Third, the first-player rule conflicts between sources. Mattel says the highest card dealt chooses the dealer and the player to the dealer's left starts. A secondary site says the youngest player goes first. Mattel is authoritative, but a digital game can simply randomize or rotate the starter. Wikipedia also suggests teams for 6 or more players, which Mattel's "2-6" box does not support. Mattel's partnership rules (shared stocks, partners may play each other's discards) can ship as an optional mode for 4 or 6 players rather than as a core rule.

## Options should be data, and hot-seat privacy matters more than any variant

Because variants are thin and low-quality in the sources, the options screen should be a typed `GameConfig` object that the engine reads, not branching code. The researchers' list is stock size (presets 10, 20 and 30, with defaults of 30 for 2-4 players and 20 for 5-6), wild count (0, 9, 18, 27), hand size (default 5, range 4-7), discard pile count and building pile count (both default 4), and the 12-clear on or off. It also covers the game mode (single round, or play to a target of 100, 250 or 500 using Mattel's 25 plus 5-per-card formula) and the seat mix of human and computer players. Mattel's commercial spin-offs (Masters with coins, Express, Junior) are not documented beyond marketing copy ([Mattel Masters](https://shop.mattel.com/products/skip-bo-masters-hjr21)), so the recommendation is not to clone them.

Existing digital clones suggest which conveniences players expect: AI difficulty levels, stock-size choice, undo and hints. One browser clone offers four AI tiers, a once-per-game undo and a "Smart Help" hint ([Docker Hub lx-games](https://hub.docker.com/r/lxtools/lx-games)). Another listing advertises 3 difficulty levels and selectable stockpile size ([rawg.io](https://rawg.io/games/skip-bo-free)). Undo and hint are cheap to build once the engine is a pure reducer over immutable state. The feature specific to this project is shared-screen play. With several humans on one display, the game needs a "pass the device" interstitial that hides the current hand between turns, plus a toggle for whether opponents' hands are visible. The notes flag this as a design inference and did not research it. It is likely more important to the family-night use case than any rules variant.

## Cartoony art comes from one SVG card function, with color never carrying meaning alone

Draw every card in code from a single `drawCard(value, size)` function on a 5:7 card (a 100x140 viewBox is the suggested size), then cache the result per size. The suggested anatomy is a cream body, a 3px dark navy outline, an inset colored panel, a large number with a thick `paint-order: stroke` outline, corner badges, a glossy highlight arc and a hard offset drop shadow. The researchers could not find an authoritative description of Mattel's real artwork or band ranges. The decision is to invent an original scheme rather than copy one: three bands (1-4, 5-8, 9-12) plus a wild, each with its own shape badge (circle, diamond, star) and a distinct wild glyph with a "SKIP" ribbon. Do not reproduce Mattel's logo or typography. Mattel's current edition added symbols to identify card colors for colorblind players, which the notes source only to a retailer listing ([desertcart](https://www.desertcart.com.cy/products/771767)). That makes redundant coding both accessible and conventional.

Accessibility drives the palette. The recommendation is to base the three bands on the Okabe-Ito colorblind-safe palette ([thematic docs](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/thematic/html/okabe_ito.html)): sky blue `#56B4E9`, bluish green `#009E73` and vermillion `#D55E00`, with a purple/pink and gold wild. These differ in luminance as well as hue. Pair color with shape badges and a low-contrast margin pattern, with a high-contrast option. WCAG sets the thresholds: 3:1 for UI components and graphics ([W3C 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/non-text-contrast)) and 4.5:1 for normal text ([Stark](https://www.getstark.co/wcag-explained/perceivable/distinguishable/contrast-minimum/)). The notes caution that no real color-vision-deficiency simulation was run on the proposed palette, so do that early. For the table, use a green felt radial gradient (`#1F7A5A` to `#0E3B3A`) with a wooden rim. Offer blue and purple themes. For type, bundle a rounded open-license web font such as Fredoka or Nunito, because no truly rounded font is reliably present on both Mac and Windows ([SitePoint](https://www.sitepoint.com/eight-definitive-font-stacks-3/) documents only the `system-ui` fallback idea). Verify the font licenses when bundling.

Layout uses a fixed logical stage (1920x1080 is the suggestion) scaled uniformly to the window, with unit `u` equal to viewport height over 100. The local seat sits at the bottom with a fanned hand. Opponents sit around an ellipse: top for 2 players, top-left and top-right for 3, then left, top and right for 4. Five and six players add the remaining ring positions, and their hands shrink to a card-back fan with a count. Each seat gets a name pill, a stock-count badge and a turn ring. The center holds the four building piles and the draw pile. Provide a UI-scale setting from 80% to 150%.

Choose DOM plus SVG plus CSS as the renderer, with GSAP for choreography (its Flip plugin handles cards moving between piles). A card game has dozens of elements, not thousands, so DOM gives crisp vector output at any pixel ratio, free text layout and hit-testing, and, importantly, assertable state through Playwright locators. The notes mark this as judgment and found no benchmarks. The npm registry lists gsap 3.15.0 and pixi.js 8.22.0 ([npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/)). The notes did not verify GSAP's current license, so check it before depending on it. Web Animations API or CSS keyframes are the zero-dependency fallback. If confetti and pile-clear sparkles outgrow the DOM, a transparent Pixi canvas overlay is the escape hatch. Animate only `transform` and `opacity`, and pool particles.

The "juice" recipes are all tunable starting values. Deal cards with an 80 ms stagger over 350 ms along a slight arc, and let the 12-card pile clear fan out with a sparkle burst of 30-60 particles. Add a 2-3 px shake and a two-frame hit-stop. These are modest by design, since the cited game-feel sources stress restraint ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/squeezing-more-juice-out-of-your-game-design-), [Wayline](https://www.wayline.io/learn/game-feel/1)). Honor `prefers-reduced-motion` ([MDN mirror](https://docs.w3cub.com/css/@media/prefers-reduced-motion)) by replacing flights with 100 ms fades, and also provide in-game toggles for shake and particles.

## Every sound is synthesized, and the game logic only emits events

The sound set is about 20 events, split into frequent tactile sounds (noise bursts through filters, quiet and slightly randomized) and rare rewards (tonal chimes and fanfares). The tactile set is shuffle, deal, draw, pick-up, place, discard and invalid. The reward and UI set is wild, pile-complete, turn-change, win, lose and button sounds. A card "tap" is a lowpass-filtered noise burst of about 60 ms plus a sine dropping from 180 to 90 Hz. The pile-complete reward is a pentatonic run into a held C-major chord with a noise sweep, about 1.6 seconds in all. The invalid sound is two descending low triangle blips, deliberately gentle. The notes are explicit that these frequencies and envelopes are the researchers' own starting points. The sourced material covers only Web Audio mechanics: noise from a random-filled `AudioBuffer`, enveloping with scheduled `AudioParam` ramps ([MDN Advanced techniques](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Web_Audio_API/Advanced_techniques)), and the sfxr-style parameter vocabulary of wave type, envelope, arpeggio and filters ([jsfxr](https://github.com/chr15m/jsfxr)). Plan a tuning-by-ear pass. Repeated sounds get plus or minus 3-5% pitch and plus or minus 10% gain jitter so they do not sound machine-gunned, and hover sounds need rate limiting.

A nice musical idea is escalating pitch. Keep a streak counter of consecutive plays in a turn and step the tonal layer up a major-pentatonic scale, capped at an octave and reset on turn change, invalid move or pile completion. Mapping card number to scale degree makes a pile "sing" upward from 1 to 12 and resolve on the octave. Background music is a generative 4-bar loop at about 80 bpm in C major (I-vi-IV-V). It uses soft triangle pads and a sparse random-walk pentatonic pluck through a light feedback delay, scheduled with the standard lookahead pattern on the audio clock ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Web_Audio_API/Advanced_techniques)). Route SFX and music through separate gain buses (suggested defaults 0.8 and 0.25) into a master gain and a `DynamicsCompressorNode` used as a limiter. The spec's default compressor settings are threshold -24 dB, knee 30 and ratio 12 ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/DynamicsCompressorNode)), and the notes suggest tightening the threshold to about -10 dB. Mute by ramping bus gain, and duck the music about 3 dB under fanfares. Raw Web Audio is enough, so Howler (listed at 2.2.4 on npm) is unnecessary for a no-samples design. Tone.js was not evaluated.

Two gotchas matter in Electron. Contexts created outside a user gesture start suspended, so create or resume the `AudioContext` on the first click or key press ([MDN Best practices](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Web_Audio_API/Best_practices), [Chrome](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/autoplay)). A desktop game always has that first click on its menu. Community reports disagree about whether `autoplayPolicy` or a command-line switch reliably helps in Electron ([Electron switches docs](https://www.electronjs.org/docs/latest/api/command-line-switches)), so treat the gesture-based resume as the guaranteed path and the setting as belt-and-braces. Also build noise buffers from `ctx.sampleRate`, since 44.1 and 48 kHz differ across machines, and never ramp an exponential envelope to exactly 0.

## One AI engine with three difficulty settings

No documented Skip-Bo AI implementation exists in the sources. The only precedent is the four-tier description from the Web-Bo clone: Easy makes mistakes and plays defensively, Medium plays standard strategy and saves jokers, Hard plays aggressively and uses discards efficiently, and Ultimate plans ahead ([Docker Hub lx-games](https://hub.docker.com/r/lxtools/lx-games)). Strategy advice across low-quality sources converges on four ideas: favor the stock pile, hold wilds, organize discards and avoid feeding the next player ([BoardGaming.com](https://boardgaming.com/gametips/skip-bo), [Diabolical Plots](https://www.diabolicalplots.com/tabletop-game-review-skip-bo/)). Nothing here is rigorously validated, and the notes found no solved or simulated analysis. Treat the design below as hypotheses to confirm by self-play.

The recommended structure is one search-and-score engine parameterized by weights, error rate and search depth, so tiers differ in numbers rather than code. A turn is a sequence of plays that ends with a forced discard. Branching is small: about 5 hand cards, the stock top and four discard tops, each aimed at up to four building piles. So the Hard tier can depth-first search the orderings of plays within a turn, score each end state after the mandatory discard and pick the best. The scoring function rewards stock plays and reduced stock distance, and rewards hand-emptying plays for the extra draw. It rewards keeping an empty discard pile and building orderly, descending discard piles. It penalizes spending a wild, and penalizes burying a card the stock will need. It also penalizes raising a building pile to N-1 when an opponent's visible stock top is N, scaled by that opponent's remaining stock. Because every stock top is visible on a shared screen, the AI can compute that last penalty exactly. An optional upgrade is Monte Carlo rollouts using the Medium policy over the unseen-card distribution. Medium is a fixed greedy priority list: stock plays first, then plays that enable the stock card, then discard-top plays, then hand plays, wilds last. Easy plays a random legal move with probability around 0.5-0.7, stops early, discards at random and ignores blocking. Add a think delay and visible move animation so computer turns are readable.

Calibrate with self-play tournaments. The suggested targets are Hard beating Medium and Medium beating Easy more than 70% of the time. This doubles as a regression test for the AI.

## Toolchain, packaging and a layered test strategy

Versions were checked against the live npm registry on 2026-10-30. Electron 45.2.0 (Chromium M156, Node 24.22) is current, and Electron supports the latest three majors on an 8-week cadence, so 42 is already end-of-life and 46 is tentatively due in January 2027 ([Electron schedule](https://releases.electronjs.org/schedule), [timelines](https://www.electronjs.org/docs/latest/tutorial/electron-timelines)). Scaffold with `npm create @quick-start/electron@latest`, which uses electron-vite ([guide](https://electron-vite.org/guide/)). electron-vite 6.0.0 pairs with Vite 8 ([changelog](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/alex8088/electron-vite/master/CHANGELOG.md)). Pin TypeScript 6 for now. TypeScript 7 (the native Go compiler) went stable in July 2026 but has no stable programmatic API until 7.1, which tools that import `typescript` depend on ([InfoQ](https://infoq.com/news/2026/08/typescript-7-released/)). The notes treat the claim that Vite and Vitest are unaffected as the researchers' own inference. Using `tsc` only as a type-checker is the safe place to try 7. Keep Electron's secure defaults: context isolation, sandbox, no Node integration, a strict CSP, denied window opens and navigation, denied permission requests, and the `runAsNode` fuse off in release builds ([Electron security](https://www.electronjs.org/docs/latest/tutorial/security)). An offline game can afford `default-src 'none'`. One unverified point to check: sandboxed preloads may not support ESM, so confirm what electron-vite emits.

Source layout should put all rules in `src/shared`, which is pure TypeScript with no DOM or Electron imports. Settings can be a tiny atomic-write JSON file in `userData` instead of the ESM-only electron-store 12. Save window bounds from `getNormalBounds()` (the notes flag this API detail as unverified) and re-validate them against connected displays. Fullscreen uses `setFullScreen`, which is asynchronous on macOS ([BrowserWindow](https://www.electronjs.org/docs/latest/api/browser-window)).

Packaging uses electron-builder 26.x. Version 27 is still alpha and changes config schema and publishing defaults ([electron.build CLI](https://www.electron.build/docs/cli/)). Build a macOS universal DMG with `electron-builder --mac --universal`, which merges x64 and arm64 builds with roughly twice the size ([architecture docs](https://www.electron.build/v26/docs/architecture)). That is acceptable because the game has no native modules. Build Windows NSIS and portable targets on a GitHub Actions `windows-latest` runner. The official CI example is exactly this matrix. The only documented macOS-hosted Windows route is Wine in Docker, which is untested on Apple Silicon and Wine is not installed on the build machine. Signing is a real decision. Unsigned arm64 Mac builds probably need at least an ad-hoc signature (`identity: "-"`), and unsigned Windows installers show SmartScreen warnings. Both points come from the researchers' experience, not a source. Distributing beyond family therefore means an Apple Developer ID, notarization (`@electron/notarize` 3.1.2) and, optionally, a Windows certificate. The notes could not retrieve the full dmg, nsis and notarize option docs, so validate that config against the current schema.

Testing is layered by cost. Most tests run in Vitest 5 on plain Node against the pure engine: property-style invariants such as 162 cards conserved across all zones, no illegal pile sequences and no pile past 12. Add scenario tests for each Mattel rule and edge case, including wild completion, hand refill mid-turn, recycling and the empty-draw-pile decision. Seeded shuffles make every game reproducible. AI tests assert only legal moves, and a tournament harness checks the tier ordering. Audio splits into three parts. Recipes are written as pure data (for example a pile-complete function returning `{t, freq, dur}` notes), so tests can assert every note is in scale and the total is under 2 seconds. A `FakeSoundManager` asserts event order, such as `place` then `pileComplete`, and streak reset on an invalid move. A recording mock `AudioContext` checks that every voice has a scheduled `stop`, that no envelope ramps exponentially to zero, and that `resume()` is called when suspended. `OfflineAudioContext` renders can check peak amplitude below 1.0 and non-silence ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Web_Audio_API/Best_practices)). The notes did not evaluate any ready-made audio mock library.

Playwright drives the Electron shell with `_electron.launch({ args: ['.'] })`, `firstWindow()` and `page.screenshot()` ([Electron testing guide](https://www.electronjs.org/docs/latest/tutorial/automated-testing)), though Electron support is labelled experimental. Playwright 1.64 bundles Chromium 156, matching Electron 45. Keep one pitfall in mind. Launches can time out if the `nodeCliInspect` fuse is disabled ([Playwright Electron API](https://playwright.dev/docs/api/class-electron)), so flip that fuse only in release builds. For deterministic runs, use an environment flag to seed the RNG, fix the window size and scale factor, redirect `userData` to a temp directory, and expose a test state hook. These practices are the researchers' unverified experience. Add a second smoke project that launches the packaged `--dir` output via `executablePath` to catch asar and asset-path errors. Because the UI is DOM, E2E tests assert on piles and counts directly, while screenshots serve as a visual regression aid. There is no true headless Electron mode. macOS CI needs a logged-in GUI session, and Linux would need xvfb.

## Conclusion

The rules are the easiest part of this project. They are short, consistent across three Mattel sheets, and nearly fully specified. The real risks are the unsourced craft layers: the AI weights, the sound tuning, the animation timings and the palette. The research supplies structure and starting values for these but no ground truth. The architecture therefore should make tuning cheap. That means data-driven configs and AI weight tables, recipe-as-data sounds, seeded determinism and self-play harnesses, so judgment calls can be tested and revised quickly instead of baked into code.

Two findings reshape the obvious plan. Shared-screen play makes hand-hiding and pass-the-device flow a first-class feature, which no variant or competitor research covered. And the distribution decision (family-only versus public release) determines whether signing and notarization costs and a Windows CI pipeline are required now or can wait. Resolve that early. Open verification items worth a short spike before committing are the CVD simulation of the palette, GSAP's license, sandboxed-preload output format in electron-vite 6, and the empty-draw-pile rule behavior.
